The Brazilian fresh produce export calendar

Which Brazilian fruit is in season when, why the southern-hemisphere calendar matters to northern buyers, and where the genuine year-round lines sit.

Most buyers come to Brazil for one of two reasons. Either they want counter-seasonal supply — fruit at a moment when their usual origin has nothing — or they want a line that runs all year and does not need re-tendering every quarter. Brazil can do both, but not with the same products, and the difference is worth understanding before you build a programme around it.

Why the southern hemisphere matters

Brazil straddles the equator. That single fact does more work than any other in explaining its export calendar. The far south has a genuine temperate season; the northeast, close to the equator and heavily irrigated, behaves almost like a year-round greenhouse.

So "Brazilian season" is not one thing. A mango out of the São Francisco Valley and an apple out of Santa Catarina are on entirely different clocks.

Treat those windows as the shape of a normal year rather than a promise. Weather moves them. A late wet season in the northeast can push a mango programme back by two or three weeks, and there is no paperwork that fixes that.

The genuine year-round lines

Three products are close to continuous: papaya, Tahiti lime and banana. If you need a line you can hold a shelf position with, start there.

That said, "year-round" means fruit is available, not that price is flat. Lime in particular swings hard. In the Brazilian wet season, volume tightens and price follows. Buyers who lock a fixed price across twelve months without a review clause tend to discover this in February.

Where the counter-seasonal value sits

The strongest counter-seasonal case is grape and mango into Europe and North America. Brazilian seedless grape arrives when the northern-hemisphere crop is finished and before the main southern-hemisphere alternatives are at full volume. That window is narrow and competitive, and it rewards buyers who commit early rather than shopping it week by week.

Planning against the calendar

A few habits separate programmes that work from programmes that get cancelled:

  • Commit ahead of the window, not inside it. Growers allocate volume before harvest. Arriving at peak with a spot enquiry gets you whatever is left.
  • Fix a quality specification, not just a price. Calibre, brix and pulp temperature at loading matter more to your arrival quality than a small difference in FOB.
  • Build in a weather clause. Everyone in this trade knows harvest slips. Contracts that pretend otherwise create disputes rather than preventing them.
  • Ask which farm. A region is not a specification. The valley grows excellent fruit and ordinary fruit.
