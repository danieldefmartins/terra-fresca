Seedless grapes from the São Francisco Valley

An irrigated valley in the Brazilian semi-arid that can harvest twice a year and fill a window when almost nobody else can. What that means for a buyer building a grape programme.

The São Francisco Valley, straddling Pernambuco and Bahia in Brazil's northeast, is one of the more unusual fresh produce regions in the world. It is semi-arid, close to the equator, and irrigated from the São Francisco river — and that combination produces something that matters commercially.

Why the region is unusual

Grapes elsewhere follow a temperate cycle: one crop a year, dictated by winter dormancy. In the São Francisco Valley there is no winter to speak of. Vines are managed through pruning and irrigation rather than by the season, which means more than one harvest per year and a degree of control over timing that temperate regions simply do not have.

High light, low humidity and reliable irrigation also mean low disease pressure and consistent sugar development.

For a buyer, the practical consequence is this: the valley can put fruit into windows that are otherwise thinly supplied, and it can do so with more scheduling flexibility than a single-harvest region.

The varieties

The valley grows the modern seedless table grape varieties European and North American retail expects:

  • Green — Sugraone, Thompson Seedless and newer selections
  • Red — Crimson Seedless, Flame Seedless
  • Black — Midnight Beauty, Autumn Royal

Variety selection in table grapes moves quickly, driven by breeding programmes and licensing. Confirm what a grower is actually planted with for the season you are buying, rather than assuming last year's mix.

The window

The principal export window runs roughly October to December, with a second, smaller harvest earlier in the year. The October window is the commercially important one, because it sits between the end of northern-hemisphere supply and the full arrival of other southern-hemisphere origins.

That window is narrow and it is contested. Growers allocate it well in advance. A buyer arriving in September looking for October volume is negotiating for what is left.

What determines arrival quality

Grapes are less chilling-sensitive than tropical fruit and are carried considerably colder, which helps. The risks are different:

  • Sulphur dioxide management. SO2 pads control decay in transit. Too little and you get botrytis; too much and you get bleaching and taint. This is a genuine craft.
  • Rachis condition. The stem browning and drying out is often what a retailer rejects on, not the berries. It is a cold-chain and humidity question.
  • Berry shatter. Loose berries in the bag are a handling and packing issue.
  • Brix at harvest. As with all fruit, the ceiling is set in the field.

Ask specifically about SO2 regime and rachis condition on arrival. Buyers who only discuss brix and calibre are discussing half the problem.

Building a programme

  • Commit early. Allocation for the main window closes before the fruit exists.
  • Agree the variety mix, not just the colour. "Red seedless" covers varieties that behave differently.
  • Specify punnet or bag format at tender. Retail packing formats are slow to change mid-season.
  • Ship from a northeastern port. The valley is far closer to Pecém and Natal than to Santos, and the transit to Europe is shorter. See choosing a Brazilian load port.

The valley's advantage is timing and control. Both of those are only useful to a buyer who commits before the window rather than during it.
