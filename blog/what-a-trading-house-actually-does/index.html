What a trading house actually does, and why it is not a broker

The difference between someone who introduces you to a grower and someone who takes ownership of the container. It decides who carries the risk when a shipment goes wrong.

"Trader", "broker", "agent" and "exporter" get used interchangeably in fresh produce, and the distinction is not pedantry. It determines who is accountable when a container arrives at 12°C instead of 8°C.

The broker model

A broker introduces a buyer to a seller and takes a commission. They do not own the fruit. They may be genuinely useful — good brokers know who has what, and that knowledge is worth paying for.

But when the container arrives short, or warm, or with the wrong paperwork, the broker's position is structurally awkward. They are not a party to the sale. They can facilitate a conversation between you and a grower on another continent, in another language, who has already been paid.

The trading house model

A trading house buys the fruit. It takes ownership at the farm gate, carries it through the cold chain, and sells it on its own account.

That changes the incentives completely:

  • The grower is paid for the fruit, on the trading house's account, whether or not the buyer is happy.
  • The buyer has one counterparty, who owns the problem.
  • The trading house carries the risk of quality, timing and price movement — which means it has every reason to pre-cool properly and get the paperwork right.

There is no version of this where the trading house is indifferent to arrival quality. It has already paid for the fruit.

What that means in practice

When you buy from a trading house you should expect:

  • One invoice, from the entity that owns the goods
  • One point of contact for sourcing, cold chain, documentation and claims
  • Named farms, because it buys from specific growers rather than an anonymous market
  • The document set assembled by the seller, not coordinated between three parties

And you should expect to pay for that. A trading house's margin covers real risk. A broker's commission does not, which is why it is smaller.

When a broker is the right answer

Honestly: when you are buying spot, occasionally, and you have your own quality control at destination and your own compliance capability. If you have those, a broker is efficient and a trading house is overhead.

The model earns its keep when you are running a programme — repeat volume, a shelf position to defend, and a retailer who will not accept "the grower says it left in good condition" as an answer.

The question to ask

There is one question that settles it:

Who owns the fruit while it is on the water?

If the answer is "the grower" or "it depends", you are dealing with a broker, whatever the letterhead says. That is fine — as long as you have priced the risk you are carrying.
