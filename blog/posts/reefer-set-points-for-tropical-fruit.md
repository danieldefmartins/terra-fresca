A reefer container does not cool cargo. It circulates air at a set temperature. That sentence sounds pedantic until the first time a load arrives with chilling injury and everyone points at the data logger, which shows the box did exactly what it was told all voyage.

## Set-point versus pulp temperature

The **set-point** is what the machine is asked to deliver at the delivery air. The **pulp temperature** is the temperature inside the fruit. They converge slowly, and a reefer is not designed to pull heat out of warm cargo quickly.

That is the single most consequential fact in this trade. A container loaded with fruit at field heat will not reach set-point in transit in any useful way. It will arrive with a cold shell and a warm core, which is how you get uneven ripening in the same pallet.

Pre-cooling is not an optimisation. It is the job.

## What actually goes wrong

Three failure modes account for most claims:

1. **Loaded warm.** Fruit goes in above spec and never recovers. The logger looks perfect.
2. **Chilling injury.** The set-point is below what the product tolerates. Damage is often invisible at loading and obvious at the retailer.
3. **Bad stow.** Air cannot circulate. Cartons block the T-floor, or the load is stacked past the red line, and the top of the container never sees cold air.

Note that only one of those is a machine fault, and it is the rarest.

## Ranges to argue from

Tropical fruit is generally intolerant of the temperatures that suit apples and pears. As a rough orientation:

- **Bananas** are the classic chilling-sensitive product and are carried notably warmer than most produce.
- **Mango, papaya and pineapple** are also chilling-sensitive; too cold produces pitting, poor colour development and off flavour.
- **Grapes** tolerate much colder carriage and are commonly shipped with sulphur dioxide pads for decay control.
- **Limes** sit in between and will show pitting and rind breakdown if carried too cold.

Do not take those as set-points. The correct figure depends on variety, maturity at harvest, transit time and destination protocol, and a good shipper will specify it per programme rather than per product. Ask for the number in writing and ask what it is based on.

## Controlled atmosphere

For long transits, controlled atmosphere — lowering oxygen and raising carbon dioxide — slows respiration and extends shelf life. It is not free, it is not available on every vessel, and it is not a substitute for pre-cooling. It is worth specifying on long-haul programmes where arrival shelf life is the constraint, and it is money wasted on a short run.

## Practical checks for a buyer

You can catch most problems with four questions:

- What was the **pulp temperature at loading**, measured how, and by whom?
- Can I see the **full logger download**, not a summary graph?
- What was the **stow pattern**, and was the load kept below the red line?
- Was the container **pre-tripped**, and is there a record?

A supplier who has these to hand is telling you something about how they run. A supplier who has to go and ask is telling you something too.
