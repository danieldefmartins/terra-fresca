Every consignment of fresh plant material leaving Brazil travels with a phytosanitary certificate issued by **MAPA**, the Ministry of Agriculture and Livestock. Buyers see it in the document pack, sign for it, and mostly never think about it until something goes wrong at the destination border.

It is worth understanding, because it is the document most likely to strand your cargo.

## What it actually attests

A phytosanitary certificate is a statement by the exporting country's plant protection organisation that the consignment has been inspected and is considered free from quarantine pests, and that it conforms to the importing country's plant health requirements.

Two things follow from that definition, and both surprise people:

- It is **not a quality certificate**. It says nothing about brix, calibre, colour or condition. Fruit can be fully compliant and commercially poor.
- It is issued **against the destination's rules**. The same fruit needs a different declaration depending on where it is going.

That second point is where most problems start.

## Additional declarations

Many destinations require specific wording on the certificate — an "additional declaration" — confirming that a particular treatment, origin condition or pest-free area status applies. The exact wording matters. A certificate that is correct in substance but wrong in phrasing can still be rejected.

This is not bureaucratic pedantry from the destination side. Border officials are matching text against a rule, and they generally cannot exercise judgement about intent.

## Where it sits in the document set

For a typical ocean shipment you are looking at:

- Commercial invoice and packing list
- Bill of lading
- Phytosanitary certificate
- Certificate of origin, where a preference or a requirement calls for one
- Any destination-specific import permit or prior notice

The phytosanitary certificate is the one with a short useful life and the one that must match the physical consignment exactly. Container number, quantities, product description and origin all need to agree with the rest of the pack.

## What goes wrong

In practice, the recurring failures are mundane:

1. **Mismatch.** The container number or carton count on the certificate does not match the bill of lading.
2. **Missing additional declaration.** The destination requires specific wording; the certificate does not carry it.
3. **Wrong botanical name.** The certificate uses a name the destination's system does not recognise.
4. **Timing.** Inspection and issuance happen too far ahead of loading, or the document arrives after the vessel.
5. **Amendment attempts.** Certificates are not freely amendable after issue. Fixing an error can mean re-inspection.

None of these are exotic. All of them cost demurrage.

## What a buyer should ask for

Before the first shipment on a new programme:

- Confirmation that the exporter has **shipped this product to this destination before**, and can show the certificate wording used.
- A **draft document set** reviewed against your customs broker's requirements, before the container is loaded.
- Clarity on **who amends what** if an error is found, and who bears the cost.

> The cheapest phytosanitary problem is the one caught while the fruit is still in the packhouse.
