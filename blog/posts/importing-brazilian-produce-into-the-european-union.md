The European Union is Brazil's largest fresh produce market, and the requirements are more codified than most origins are used to. The system is coherent once you see its shape, but it does not forgive gaps.

This is an orientation, not legal advice. Confirm current requirements with the competent authority in your member state before shipping.

## Plant health control

Fresh produce entering the EU is subject to official controls at a **Border Control Post**. Consignments are pre-notified through the EU's electronic system, presented with their phytosanitary certificate, and subject to documentary, identity and physical checks at frequencies that vary by commodity and origin.

Two practical consequences:

- Your consignment must arrive at a BCP **approved for that category of goods**. Not every port handles every category.
- Pre-notification is the importer's responsibility and it is time-bound. Late notification creates delay regardless of how good the fruit is.

## Maximum residue levels

The EU sets maximum residue levels for plant protection products, and enforcement is genuinely strict. An MRL exceedance is not a commercial disagreement; it is a regulatory event that can trigger rapid alerts, increased checks on subsequent consignments from the same origin, and destruction or re-export of the cargo.

The important subtlety for buyers: **a product legally applied in Brazil may still exceed an EU MRL**, because the two regimes set limits independently. Approval at origin is not a defence at destination.

This is why the residue testing regime at the farm matters more than any certificate. Ask what is tested, at what frequency, against which standard, and by which laboratory. "We are certified" is not an answer to that question.

## Traceability

EU food law requires operators to identify their immediate suppliers and immediate customers — one step back, one step forward. That is the legal floor.

European retail applies considerably more. Expect to be asked for traceability to the **individual grower and production unit**, not the exporter, and to be able to produce it quickly during an incident. The practical test is not whether the data exists but how fast you can retrieve it under pressure.

## What European buyers ask beyond the law

- **Social compliance auditing**, commonly through a recognised scheme
- **Certification with buyer-specific add-ons**, agreed at tender rather than retro-fitted
- **Packaging and labelling** aligned to the destination market, including country of origin and lot identification
- **Sustainability reporting**, increasingly including transport and cold-chain energy

None of these are optional in practice, whatever the regulation says.

## Practical sequence

1. Confirm the commodity's **plant health requirements** and any additional declaration wording.
2. Identify a **suitable BCP** and confirm your entry point handles the category.
3. Agree **pre-notification responsibility** in writing — importer or agent.
4. Review the grower's **residue programme against EU MRLs**, not Brazilian ones.
5. Test your **traceability recall** before you need it.

> The EU system rewards preparation and punishes improvisation. Almost every problem is visible weeks before the vessel sails.
