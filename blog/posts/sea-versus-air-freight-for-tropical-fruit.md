The usual framing is that sea is cheap and slow, air is fast and expensive, and you choose based on urgency. That framing is not wrong, but it hides the decisions that actually matter.

## Air changes what you can harvest

The real difference is not speed. It is **maturity at harvest**.

Fruit destined for a three-week ocean transit has to be picked firm enough to survive it. That constrains how ripe it can be, which constrains how it eats on arrival. Fruit going by air can be picked closer to eating ripeness, because it will be on a shelf within days.

For products where the gap between "ships well" and "tastes best" is wide — soft tropicals in particular — that is a genuine quality difference, not a marginal one.

## What air is actually for

Air earns its cost in four situations:

1. **Products that cannot survive the sea transit** at acceptable quality.
2. **Season openers and closers** — the first and last fruit of a window, when price is at its highest.
3. **Promotional gaps**, where a retailer has committed to a date and the vessel will not make it.
4. **Samples and programme qualification**, where the point is to show what the fruit can be.

It is not for filling a routine programme. Nobody has built a sustainable business air-freighting commodity volume.

## What changes operationally

Air is not sea with a different vehicle. It changes the whole operation:

- **Packaging.** Volumetric weight drives cost, so packaging is lighter and cartons are configured differently. You are paying for air in the box.
- **Cold chain gaps.** Air freight has more handling touchpoints without power — apron time, transfers, terminal handling. Paradoxically, the fast mode often has the *weaker* continuous cold chain.
- **Lead times.** Airline capacity for perishables is booked, and belly capacity is seasonal.
- **Documentation.** The phytosanitary certificate still has to be right, and there is far less slack in the schedule to fix an error.

That third point about the cold chain surprises people. A container is a temperature-controlled box for the whole voyage. An air shipment is a series of cold rooms with warm gaps between them.

## Where the economics sit

The honest answer is that the ratio moves. Air freight rates are volatile, they respond to passenger capacity, and fuel moves them. Any specific multiple quoted in an article is out of date by the time you read it.

What is stable is the **shape** of the decision:

- If the product is low-value per kilo, sea wins and it is not close.
- If the product is high-value per kilo *and* time-sensitive, air can work.
- If the value is in hitting a date rather than in the fruit itself, price the cost of missing the date before you decide.

Ask for both quoted against the same programme, on the same week, and compare landed cost per saleable kilo rather than freight cost per kilo. Air shipments often have lower waste, which closes some of the gap.

> Choose the mode against the product and the window. A rule that says "always sea" is as lazy as one that says "always air".
