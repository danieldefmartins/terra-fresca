Brazil is not a country you ship *from*. It is a country you ship from a **specific place** in, and the distance between those places is continental. Santos to Pecém is roughly the distance from Lisbon to Warsaw.

For fresh produce, that matters more than it does for almost any other cargo, because every hour of inland trucking is an hour of shelf life.

## The main options

**Santos (São Paulo).** The largest port in Latin America, with the deepest carrier coverage and the most sailings to the widest range of destinations. If a service exists to your market, it probably calls at Santos. The trade-off is congestion, and its distance from the northeastern growing regions.

**Itajaí and Navegantes (Santa Catarina).** Strong reefer handling and the natural outlet for southern production — apple, and southern-grown lines. Generally less congested than Santos.

**Pecém (Ceará) and Natal (Rio Grande do Norte).** The northeastern ports, and the right answer for irrigated northeastern production — melon, mango, and the São Francisco Valley grape crop. Critically, they are **closer to Europe** than the southern ports, which shortens transit.

**Salvador (Bahia).** Serves Bahian production, including papaya and mango from the north of the state.

## Match the port to the farm, not to the habit

The failure mode is straightforward: an exporter has a relationship with a forwarder in Santos, so everything goes through Santos, including melon grown 2,000 km north.

That fruit is now spending two extra days on a truck before it sees a reefer plug. Nothing downstream recovers it. The cold chain was compromised before the container was even loaded.

If your programme is northeastern fruit going to Europe, the northeastern ports are usually the answer, and a shipper who insists otherwise should be able to explain why in terms of vessel schedules rather than convenience.

## What to weigh

| Factor | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Distance from the packhouse | Direct shelf-life cost, and reefer plug time on the truck |
| Sailing frequency | A cheaper rate on a weekly service can lose to a pricier twice-weekly one |
| Transit time to destination | Northeast to Europe is materially shorter than south to Europe |
| Congestion risk | A missed connection on fresh produce is not a delay, it is a claim |
| Reefer plug capacity | Ports differ in how many plugged containers they can hold, and for how long |

## Transit time is not the whole story

Buyers fixate on port-to-port transit. The number that determines arrival quality is **packhouse to plug** — how long the fruit spends between leaving controlled temperature and being connected on the vessel or at the terminal.

Ask your supplier for that figure. Many cannot produce it, which is itself informative.

## A note on air

For fruit where the window is genuinely short, air freight out of São Paulo (GRU) or Fortaleza changes the calculus entirely. It is expensive and it is not a default, but for a high-value line arriving into a tight promotional window it can be the only thing that works. We cover the trade-off in [sea versus air freight](/blog/sea-versus-air-freight-for-tropical-fruit/).

Terra Fresca ships out of Santos, Itajaí, Pecém and Natal, and chooses per programme rather than per habit.
